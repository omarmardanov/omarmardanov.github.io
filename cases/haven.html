Haven — an iOS app for the documents a household actually loses

Personal project. Product, design and SwiftUI build, solo. Runs on device; not released to the App Store, on purpose.

This one started as my own problem. I have moved countries several times, and each move adds a layer: a residence permit here, a tax number there, a birth certificate that matters in one country and is a curiosity in the next, an expiry date nobody warns you about. The documents were not lost, exactly. They were in the phone gallery, in a Telegram saved-messages thread, in a folder somewhere in the flat, and in an email attachment from 2021 — and finding the right one always happened at the worst possible moment.

The interesting question was whether that is an immigrant problem or a general one. So before designing anything, I went and checked.

What the research said, and how it changed the product

It is general. 75% of people admit their important information is not organized, and 92% have had the experience of not finding a document exactly when they needed it. Only about 30% of households are prepared, document-wise, for an emergency. Those numbers describe people who have never emigrated anywhere.

The more useful finding was what people actually lose. It is not the file. Scanner apps — Genius Scan, CamScanner, Adobe Scan — already solve capture, and solve it well. What none of them solve is where to look six months later: they organize by capture date, not by meaning. And password managers, which people reach for by analogy, are built for credentials and have nothing to say about a will, a lease, or the instructions for the person who feeds the cat.

What gets lost is the link between a document and its context: where the paper original physically is, when it expires, and whose document it is. That reframing is the whole product.

The blue folder on the top shelf

The digital copy and the paper original live in two separate worlds that never meet. Every guide tells you to keep originals somewhere safe, and not one of them connects that location back to the scan you took. So a document in Haven carries where its paper version is kept, in plain words — "blue folder, top shelf" — alongside the scan, the expiry date, and the person it belongs to.

It is a small field. It is also the answer to the actual question people ask themselves, which is never "where is the PDF" but "where is the original, and do I need to bring it".

Organized by who and what, not by folder

Documents hang off objects: family members, property, pets. There is no folder tree. A category taxonomy exists underneath (identity, medical, financial, property, travel), drawn from where external sources agree and adapted to a post-Soviet plus international reality where a residence permit, a visa, a tax number and a pension number are all normal things to own. But the surface people navigate is the household, because that is how they remember: not "financial documents", but "the car" and "my daughter".

Refused: appliances

I added an "appliances" object type and removed it the same day. It failed exactly the test that had rejected bills and insurance policies an hour earlier: too few documents per object — a receipt and a warranty, often just the receipt — so the shelf of objects fills up with cards reading "1 document". And the pain is not there. A laptop does not have a piece of paper that goes missing in a drawer.

It got in because in my original notes "appliances" sat next to "purchases" and described a category of documents, not an object. I lifted it out without re-applying the filter I had just written down. There was even an indirect signal I ignored: there was no reasonable system icon for it, and I ended up using a plug adapter.

Filed as a rule, not a one-off: when a candidate object arrives from a list of document categories, run the object test on it again.

Refused: a "custom name" row in onboarding

Onboarding asks you to tick objects off a checklist. I noticed the main screen lets you create an object with a free-form name while the checklist only offers presets, and nearly added a "custom name" row to close the gap.

It turned out the gap wasn't real: a preset only sets the object type and the hint text, and the name itself is typed freely on the next step. Tick "other property", call it "boat". The two paths already meet.

So the row stayed out, for three reasons. The checklist is a checklist — a tap means a tick, and a row that opens a text field breaks that pattern and slows down the fastest path through the screen. Onboarding's job is not to inventory a household but to create one object and demonstrate the model, then let go. And the asymmetry: adding an item later is cheap, removing one people have got used to is not.

The same pass did find a real hole and closed it: pets had no escape hatch at all, so a parrot or a tortoise could not be entered in onboarding by any route.

Native by default

One rule governed the interface: everything is built from what the platform gives — system containers, transitions, materials, spacing, animations — and customization is a separate, deliberate decision made only after the native version has been seen on screen and judged insufficient. Not the other way round.

It is a cheap rule that pays twice. The app inherits accessibility, dynamic type and every future OS refinement for free, and design attention goes to the parts that are actually this product's problem, not to re-drawing a navigation bar.

Status

Built and running on device, developed with AI-assisted workflows and verified by testing on a real iPhone instead of by reading code. Not on the App Store: paying Apple's developer fee was declined at the start as a decision, not an oversight — the app is for my household, and a store listing adds review, screenshots, a privacy questionnaire and an annual bill to a product that has exactly one user.

What I would want before that changes: someone outside my household putting their own documents in it, which is a different kind of work from building it.
